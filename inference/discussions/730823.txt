Is That Matchup Table Measuring the Deck—or the Player? (Part 1)

This is both a follow-up to, and a correction of, my previous post: “Tracking 3,057 teams through 6 weeks of meta.”

In the previous post, I built a pooled matchup table from every game in the top band and wrote:

“Garchomp currently has the highest EV against the actual field. It may be the next bubble candidate.”

That was wrong. Garchomp was the deck being played by the strongest players in the top band.

Was its win rate high because the deck was strong, or because the people using it were strong? A pooled matchup table cannot distinguish between the two. After adjusting for pilot strength, Garchomp’s expected win rate against the field fell from 54.2% to 50.6%.

The reverse also happens. Majkel1337, ranked second in estimated skill with 13,175 games, and Yushin Ito, ranked third with 15,081 games, were both playing Alakazam—a deck whose adjusted field win rate was only 47.6%. Their strong results made Alakazam look better than it actually was.

The same bias should affect the matchup tables commonly shared in the community. So I tried to separate the two effects.

Data: 41 consecutive days from June 16 to July 26, 2026, with no missing dates. I downloaded every Kaggle Daily Top Episodes dataset and classified every episode without subsampling. The final dataset contains 209,772 completed games, 3,322 teams, and 15 archetypes. These datasets contain only episodes with high average participant ratings, so they are not a random sample of all games. Everything below applies only within the top band.

1. Model

Treating each completed game as one row, I model a game between team $i$ using deck $a$ and team $j$ using deck $b$ as

Pr(i≻j)=σ(si−sj+Mab+zSab).

Here, $i \succ j$ means that team $i$ defeats team $j$. The variable $z$ is $+1$ when $i$ goes first and $-1$ when $j$ goes first.

The parameter $s_i$ is team strength—not the human player’s skill, but the strength of the submitted agent. The parameter $M_{ab}$ is the deck matchup effect, and $S_{ab}$ is the value of going first in that matchup. All parameters are expressed in log-odds.

Why both $M$ and $S$ are necessary

Let $\mu_{ab}$ denote the log-odds that deck $a$ defeats deck $b$ when $a$ goes first. The situations represented by $\mu_{ab}$ and $\mu_{ba}$ are different, so there is no direct constraint linking them. In other words, $\mu$ is a free $K\times K$ matrix.

This matrix can always be decomposed into antisymmetric and symmetric components:

The left term, $M_{ab}$, is antisymmetric. The right term, $S_{ab}$, is symmetric.

$M$: the matchup averaged over turn order
$S$: how valuable it is to go first in that particular pairing

The antisymmetry of $M$ is unavoidable: if the roles are reversed, the win probabilities must be complementary. By contrast, $S$ is symmetric. If A benefits from going first against B, B may also benefit from going first against A.

If $S$ is omitted, first-player effects contaminate the estimated $M$. Suppose deck $a$ goes first with probability $p$. The observed win rate is

Wab=pσ(Mab+Sab)+(1−p)σ(Mab−Sab).

2. Why the Two Effects Can Be Separated: Deck Switchers

This is the most interesting part.

Suppose every team used only one deck. Add $+0.5$ to the $s_i$ of every Garchomp player, then subtract the same amount from Garchomp’s matchup row. Not a single predicted game probability changes.

More generally, for any $c\in\mathbb{R}^K$,

si↦si+ca(i),Mab↦Mab−ca+cb.

Then

(si+ca)−(sj+cb)+(Mab−ca+cb)=si−sj+Mab.

There are therefore $K$ dimensions of observationally indistinguishable solutions. In this situation, the data cannot tell whether “Garchomp is strong” or “Garchomp players are strong.”

Teams that used two or more decks break this non-identifiability. A switcher must have the same $s_i$ regardless of which deck it used, forcing $c_a=c_{a'}$. As switchers connect different archetypes, these constraints propagate and eliminate the deck-specific degrees of freedom.

The non-identifiability caused by deck-specific skill shifts is resolved, up to a global additive constant, when the archetype co-usage graph is connected.
In this graph, each node is a deck, and an edge connects two decks if the same team used both.

How many days are enough?

Changing the window length gives the following diagnostic:

Window	Co-usage graph	Share of teams that switched
3 days	9/11 connected; graph split	20%
7 days, mid-July	9/13 connected; graph split	18%
7 days, late June	15/15 connected	29%
14 days, early July	13/14 connected; graph split	16%
41 days, this post	15/15 connected	42%

This is why I used all 41 days.

In short windows, two things happen at once: some archetypes become disconnected, and the fraction of teams that switched decks falls. In July, the meta concentrated heavily around Grimmsnarl and the number of active teams declined. As a result, a seven-day window that was connected in June became disconnected in July.

The amount of data required depends not only on the duration of the window, but also on the diversity of the meta during that period.

As a robustness check, I refit the model using only games in which both teams were switchers, leaving 110,804 games. Matchup estimates moved by at most ±2.3 percentage points, and most moved by less than one point.

Garchomp	4.1%	54.2%	50.6%	−3.6pt	54.2%	46.9%	+7.3pt
Alakazam	25.0%	46.5%	47.6%	+1.1pt	52.1%	43.2%	+8.9pt
Dragapult	2.3%	48.4%	49.0%	+0.6pt	49.7%	48.2%	+1.5pt

The mean estimated skill of Garchomp users was +0.520, the highest of any deck. Their opponents averaged +0.413. The reduction after adjustment is consistent with that difference.

And Garchomp was exactly the deck being used by the players near the top of the leaderboard. The causal direction is reversed: stronger players find and adopt good decks earlier, so the measured performance of those decks becomes inflated precisely during the period when everyone is looking at the table and deciding what to copy next.

However, the top four adjusted values—52.6%, 50.9%, 50.9%, and 50.6%—all lie within two percentage points. Their exact ordering is not established. The only safe conclusion is that Garchomp is no longer the clear standalone leader.

Rankings change between going first and going second

The final three columns are visible only because turn order is modeled explicitly. “Adjusted” averages over turn order, but actual win rates can differ substantially depending on who goes first.

Alakazam is estimated at 52.1% when going first and 43.2% when going second, an 8.9-point gap. Garchomp has a +7.3-point gap. Dragapult, by contrast, differs by only +1.5 points.

This means that the best deck depends on whether you expect to go first or second. When going first, Garchomp is roughly tied with Grimmsnarl. When going second, it falls to 46.9%, well below Crustle at 50.5%.

The vertical bar is the turn-order average shown in the “Adjusted” column. Crustle and Dragapult are relatively insensitive to turn order, whereas Alakazam and Garchomp show large separations. Sorting only by the going-second estimate changes the ranking.

How large are the changes?

Suppose a naive table says that A defeats B 60% of the time. Its apparent advantage over 50% is 10 percentage points. If the adjusted estimate is 57.5%, the estimate moved by 2.5 points, meaning that

2.5÷10=25%

of the apparent advantage was removed.

The scatter plot makes the pattern clear. Cells with many games, shown in dark blue, tend to lie near the 45-degree line. Sparse cells, shown in gray, are pulled toward the horizontal 50% line.

Binning cells into non-overlapping ranges gives the following result. The number of cells is shown in parentheses, and cells with fewer than 10 games are excluded.

The amount of movement decreases with sample size. Cells with $N\ge4{,}000$ barely move, whereas cells with $N<150$ move by a median of 20.8 percentage points.

There are still exceptions among large cells. The largest is Lucario versus Trevenant, with $N=5{,}235$ and a +9.1-point change. The next is Alakazam versus Tarountula, with $N=5{,}153$ and a +7.0-point change.

These two are not shrinkage artifacts. Their sample sizes are large enough that the ridge penalty has little influence. The movement instead reflects a genuine difference in the skill distributions of the players using the two decks. In the figure, these are the dark points far from the 45-degree line.

One might argue that sparse raw win rates are simply noisy. But that is not the whole explanation. Between 89% and 100% of the changes point toward 50%, and their magnitude is three to seven times larger than the binomial standard error of the raw win rate. Under random sampling noise, the directions should be roughly balanced and the magnitudes should be comparable to the standard error. The pattern is systematic shrinkage.

The appropriate cutoff depends on the use case, but as a rough guideline, a cell with fewer than 1,000 games should be interpreted more as “we still do not know” than as a reliable numerical estimate. Even the 1,000–4,000 range moves by a median of 7.8 points, so crossing 1,000 games does not make a cell automatically trustworthy.

For the same reason, do not compare deck-level adjustment magnitudes directly with one another. Bellibolt users had below-average estimated skill, yet Bellibolt moved downward by 8.7 points after adjustment. That movement was driven by shrinkage, not by pilot strength.

When selecting a deck, evaluate it at your own skill level rather than using a raw sum of matchup effects.

Practical takeaway

Cloning a top player’s deck gives you their $M$, not their $s_i$.

Deck choice should therefore be based on the adjusted matchup matrix, not the naive one. Netdecking from a pooled table systematically overvalues decks currently used by strong players.

The same issue applies when deciding which player to imitate. Among high-volume Crustle pilots, one player has a raw non-mirror win rate of 54.1%, while another has 51.6%. The model ranks the 51.6% player higher, with estimated skill +0.443 versus +0.347, because that player faced a stronger field.

5. Does the Model Actually Help?

A single log-loss value cannot show whether both components are useful. In addition, a random game-level split places the same teams in both train and test, so it measures the ability to predict another game from known teams rather than genuine generalization.

I therefore evaluated four models under three different splits.

Model	Random 15%	Last 5 days	Unseen teams
Intercept only, 0.5	0.69315	0.69315	0.69315
Matchup only	0.67724	0.68534	0.67851
Skill only	0.68068	0.69779	0.69550
Skill + matchup	0.66603	0.68824	0.68140

The matchup component beats random guessing, 0.69315, under all three splits. The matchup component is useful.

The skill component, however, performs worse than random guessing on both the temporal split and the unseen-team split. The matchup-only model also outperforms the full skill-plus-matchup model in those two settings.

A static skill estimate pooled over 41 days therefore does not appear useful for forward prediction.

Why does this happen? How long does a matchup table remain useful? I tried to answer that next—and made another mistake, this time in the measurement procedure itself. That will be the subject of the follow-up post.

6. Limitations
No skill-by-deck interaction. If a team is weak only when using one particular deck, those losses must be absorbed either into its global skill parameter or into that deck’s matchup row.
Shrinkage in low-$N$ cells is not skill adjustment. The median change is 1.9 points for $N\ge4{,}000$ and 20.8 points for $N<150$. For sparse cells, the estimate is largely reflecting the prior or penalty.
The difference between naive and adjusted values is not “the contribution of skill.” It also contains shrinkage, opponent composition, agent resubmissions, and possible archetype misclassification. It should be interpreted only as the change induced by including team fixed effects.
Top-band data only. Games against lower-rated opponents are not included, so the model does not measure the ability to farm weaker teams.
Archetype-level, not exact-deck-list-level. Of the 226 deck constructions with at least 200 games, 86 were used by only one team and are therefore perfectly confounded with that team’s skill.
The identifiability argument covers only the non-identifiability described in Section 2. I have not verified the full rank of the design matrix.
