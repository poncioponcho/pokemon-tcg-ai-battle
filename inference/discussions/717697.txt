The concept of **sequencing **and prize mapping are important for almost every deck. Since I'm newer I'm not 100% confident I use the phrases 100% correct but I'm more correct than I'm not. ;-] **Sequencing **is knowing the right actions to take to have the most information available. Here's a very common example - you have Fezandipiti Ex (Fez) and Drakloak and Dreepy in play, and you have a Poke Pad (Pad) in hand. Let's say your goal is to find Stage 1 Drakloak, Basic Dreepy, and Basic Duskull on your turn. The less optimal sequencing is to use Poke Pad first for one of your 3 targets, then using Fez's Flip The Scripts (Draw3). For example, maybe I poke pad for Drakloak so I guarantee have 2 Drakloak abilities to use in order to see more cards, but there's a chance I find more Drakloak in my Fez's Draw 3 afterwards. Instead, if we use the generic draw of Fez first, we have the opportunity to see any of our 3 finds - if we found an item Buddy-Buddy Poffin (Poffin) off of Fez draw, we now are guaranteed to find those 3 cards we want with Poffin and Poke Pad! At this point, it could be optimal to use the Poffin and Pad now: I need to ask myself, "With the cards I now have, if I use Drakloak's ability and see a Dreepy (replace Dreepy as many times as needed with other cards).. would I have wished I saw different options?" and then decide to 'thin' the deck of cards I didn't want to see with other cards in my hand, in order to improve my chances of seeing other cards. In this example I've described, I may prefer to use my Poffin and Pad before using my 2 Drakloak's abilities, because I perceive higher value in seeing other cards that I cannot specifically search for at this point (from Magic the Gathering, searching your deck for a specific card is called 'tutor' or 'tutoring' so you may see that in PTCG too). So, the basic lesson is generic draw first, then search for specific cards. The primary exception to this rule is if you guarantee know what you need to see in your deck, you will "thin to win" aka discard your deck in order to have a better chance to randomly draw a card you're seeking. Prize mapping is basically asking, "How will I take knockouts in order to get 6 prizes this game?" People will say a series of numbers like 2-2-2 meaning "I will take 3 knockouts across 3 turns in a way that gets me two prizes (usually a 2-prize pokemon and not 2 single prize pokemon, but it depends) at a time." There's a lot of minutiae / things to focus in on around prize mapping.. usually matchup dependent. It ties in a lot with "prize trading / prize racing". Prize trading is simply knowing how many prizes you intend to give up as a result of your actions. A good trade can be taking a 2-prize knockout on Fez and losing a 1-prize Alakazam in return. This prize trading builds into and augments your prize race against your opponent. Your prize map may look like 2-2-2, and your opponent on the other end will be looking to disrupt your prize map. For example if you only need 2 prizes left to win and your opponent's board is single prizers plus one Meowth Ex, they could play Scoop Up Cyclone on Meowth to change into a single prize board state, now forcing you to play for one more turn. Some decks strategize to 'make your opponent take 7 or 8 prizes' by forcing your opponent into a 1-2-2 prize map, and then benching a 3-prizer (a mega pokemon in this format) afterwards, ensuring your opponent has to work harder to reach their goal of passing 6 prizes. Legacy Energy is good to disrupt a prize map. Having cards like Boss's Orders let's you maintain your prize map in case your targets are not being pushed to the active. Some decks can maintain a 1 prize board state for a long time Grimmsnarl Froslass is good at this) until they want to evolve into their 2 prize pokemon.

Right now I'm focused on teaching my agent to consistently make the "generically good" play instead of overfitting to rare, low-percentage scenarios that might stunt its overall decision-making. We'll see how that goes.. The tricky part is that those edge cases are really hard to describe as specific board states, and for my current deck these scenarios never happen in the early game. If I had to pick the most obvious errors, it would be over-attaching energy beyond your pokemon's attack cost (this is sometimes OK if you plan to retreat or are against a Crushing Hammer build, but it still depends), using a card that would put your deck at 0 cards, or choosing to not attack when doing so would take a knockout (IRL this is not true at all because a card called Unfair Stamp is in ~50% of decks, but this is for Dragapult decks that are not in our Kaggle meta, so I would always question if my agent decided against taking a knockout).
I've only been training my agent against ~10 decks I've seen in the top 30 piloted by a random decision policy. Still learning about the ML basics like 'save the best agent and the final agent' in order to make better agents, etc.. So I don't have strong signals about if I'm winning or not, yet. Hard question to answer.. I think almost every game is winnable (this is the ego/competitor in me speaking). This game is more random than chess, but we still constantly have the same players reaching the top placements in pokemon regionals. Of course it can be possible to brick for some turns and be behind, but so can your opponent (a well-timed Unfair Stamp + Team Rocket's Watchtower is SO oppressive for an Alakazam deck). In order to answer question in one facet, you could probably get data on your opening card hand or maybe clusters of cards that you do/don't have in your opening hand, maybe how many combined uses of Lillie's Determination, Fez, and other card draw actions were performed in a game, and draw a conclusion that you're more likely to lose if you do or don't have certain cards in your opening hand? For an Alakazam deck that doesn't have much in the way of disruption, you could probably guess you will lose if you are down 2 prizes into an opponent with a single prize board state - you're likely trading 1 for 1 and even if your opponent fails to get one attack off in one turn, they're still going to be ahead on the prize race. For my Honchkrow deck, I posit I'm more likely to lose if I don't use Team Rocket's Proton in my first 2 turns of the game (and extremely likely to lose if I use Proton beyond the first 2 turns), so that's something I've been trying to shape in my agent.

Here's a small tangent: In the limitless tournaments, good players tend to play the good decks which is why Dragapult is selected so much. Every other card in that deck lets you see so many cards, so it's really hard to have anything inconsistent with that deck. An Alakazam deck won Indianapolis because it hard teched for Dragapult which was a majority of the room, it built it's deck in a way no one else considered at the time (it had 4 Nighttime Mine, an Elgyem, and a Dedenne which are very strong 'control' element cards whereas your normal Alakazam deck is swinging for the fences). The zam decks for Kaggle are following recent Zam lists, but could probably be built better since there are so few Tera pokemon decks in the ladder and so few Team Rocket's Watchtower. Those tournaments are also in a best of 3 format, whereas Kaggle is in a Bo1 format (Japan tournaments are Bo1, data is harder to find but is available. Limitless has one that was in a close-enough format to Kaggle https://limitlesstcg.com/tournaments/547 but only has a few placements and decks).
